Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

Price Elasticity of Demand measures how sensitive the quantity demanded of a good is to a change in its price. It's a key concept for pricing strategies and understanding consumer behavior.

Elastic Demand (>1)

Demand is highly sensitive to price changes. A small price increase leads to a large drop in demand. Typical for luxury goods with many substitutes.

Inelastic Demand (<1)

Demand is not very sensitive to price changes. A large price increase leads to a small drop in demand. Common for necessities like fuel and medicine.

Unitary Elasticity (=1)

The percentage change in quantity demanded is equal to the percentage change in price. Total revenue remains constant when price changes.

PED Comparison: Short-Run vs. Long-Run

Consumer behavior adapts over time. Demand for many goods, like gasoline and airline tickets, is more elastic in the long run as people find substitutes or change habits.

The Impact of Tariffs on Foreign Demand

A tariff is a tax on imported goods. It raises the price for consumers and impacts foreign demand based on the product's price elasticity.

Government Intervention in Markets

Governments intervene in markets to correct failures, redistribute income, or protect consumers and producers. These interventions often have significant, sometimes unintended, consequences.

Price Ceilings

A maximum price set by the government. Intended to make goods affordable but often leads to shortages and lower quality.

Example: Rent control can lead to a shortage of available apartments.

Price Floors

A minimum price set by the government. Aims to protect producers but can cause surpluses.

Example: Minimum wage is a price floor for labor, which can lead to unemployment if set too high.

Excise Taxes

A tax on specific goods, often to discourage consumption (e.g., cigarettes, gasoline). The burden of the tax depends on elasticity.

If demand is inelastic, consumers pay most of the tax.

Subsidies

Government payments to businesses to lower production costs and encourage output. Can lead to overproduction.

Example: Agricultural subsidies can lower food prices but distort the market.

Import Quotas

A limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported. Protects domestic industries but raises prices for consumers.

Reduces consumer choice and can provoke retaliation from other countries.
